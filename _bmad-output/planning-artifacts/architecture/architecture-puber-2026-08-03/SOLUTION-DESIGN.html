What the system is, and the constraint that shapes it

Puber is a ride-hailing backend: riders request rides, drivers get offered them, trips run, money moves. It exists to practise production patterns against a domain you can picture rather than an abstract one — and it deliberately controls every input. No maps API, no SMS provider, no real payment rail. Every rider is a generated identifier, every location a generated coordinate.

That sounds like a simplification, and in one sense it is. But it has a sharper effect than convenience: it removes every excuse. When a driver is double-booked, there is no third-party latency to blame. The concurrency is real, the state machines are real, and the failure modes are the ones you built.

The constraint that shapes the most decisions is scale ambition without scale reality. The system must be correct at roughly thirty drivers, and must survive a stress run at twenty thousand drivers and two hundred thousand riders. Those two targets pull in opposite directions constantly, and most of the interesting decisions are about which one wins where.

The rule that emerged: correctness is designed for now, capacity is designed for later.

Every guard, every conditional update, every state machine is built for correctness from day one, because those are expensive to retrofit. Every pool size, replica count and partition number is deliberately left unset, because guessing them produces fiction and the stress run exists to produce facts.

Drawing the boundaries — and why there is no saga

The first real decision was giving each service its own database. That is conventional advice, but it immediately created a problem that took a while to see clearly.

The obvious split is by actor: a rider service owning riders, a driver service owning drivers, a matching service coordinating between them. It reads well on a diagram. But then consider what happens when a driver accepts a ride. Two facts change together — the ride becomes assigned, and the driver becomes unavailable. If those live in different databases, that single action needs a distributed transaction, or a saga with compensating actions, or an accepted window of inconsistency.

Uber hit exactly this. Their old fulfilment platform had one service owning trips and another owning driver sessions, coordinated by a saga — and their own write-up is blunt about the cost: coordination handled "in an arbitrary RPC-based mechanism", the system "internally inconsistent" between the steps of a logical transaction, and debugging across entities becoming progressively harder. Their fix, after a two-year rewrite, was not a better saga. It was to unify the transactional boundary so the two entities could be written atomically.

Whatever is written together by a single action must share one transactional owner.

Not "prefer to" — must. If a design needs two services to commit together, the boundary is drawn in the wrong place, and the answer is to move the boundary rather than to add machinery that survives it being wrong.

Applying that inverts the obvious split. matching-service owns rides and the dispatch state of drivers — their availability and current assignment — because those are what change together. driver-service keeps identity and position, which nothing else co-writes. rider-service owns no data at all; with no rider accounts in scope, it is a façade translating the public API into internal calls.

So there are two tables called drivers, in two databases, owned by two services. That looks wrong until you notice they hold disjoint facts and are never joined. One is who a driver is; the other is what a driver is doing. Different questions, different owners, different lifetimes.

The payoff is that the hottest, most concurrency-sensitive operation in the system — assigning a driver to a ride — is a single local transaction over two tables in one database. No coordination, no compensation, no partial state. Postgres does the hard part.

The dispatch core

A ride moves through six states, and the one that took longest to get right was recognising that an offer is not a match. 

REQUESTED → WAITING_MATCH → OFFERED → MATCHED → IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETED
                   ↑______________|  declined, or the offer expired
                   ↑_________________________|  driver went silent before pickup

The first draft collapsed OFFERED and MATCHED into one state, on the reasoning that the ride "has a driver" in both. That is wrong in a way that only shows up later: the sweep responsible for expiring unanswered offers then has no way to tell which rides have an outstanding offer and which have an accepted one. It would either expire rides a driver had already committed to, or never expire anything.

Splitting them fixed three things at once. The expiry sweep gets a clean predicate. The driver's own status collapses to three values, because the ride now carries the distinction. And the rider-facing rule — show me who is coming — becomes true as written, since a rider should not see a driver who has not yet agreed and might decline.

The race that actually matters

A rider can cancel while a driver is accepting. Both are legal, and they arrive at the same moment. If both succeed you get a cancelled ride with a driver who believes they are on it — and nothing to reconcile it, because the ride is terminal and no sweep looks at terminal rides.

The mechanism that prevents it is not a lock, a queue or a coordinator. It is that both operations write the same row with a guard on its current state: 

UPDATE rides SET status = ? WHERE id = ? AND status = ? AND driver_id = ?

The second transaction blocks on the first's row lock, then re-evaluates its predicate against the committed row, finds the state it expected is gone, and matches zero rows. Zero rows means rejected — never "retry until it works", never "assume success".

Every state transition in the system is a conditional update carrying its expected prior state.

This is why there is no interface in front of the database. The correctness guarantee is Postgres's behaviour — row locks, predicate re-evaluation, partial unique indexes. A mock repository has none of it, so tests against one would pass while the real system double-books drivers. Tests run against the real thing.

The same principle handles the rule that a rider may only have one ride in flight. The naive version reads then writes, and races with itself. The version that works is a partial unique index over the active states — the database refuses the second insert, and the API turns that refusal into a 409. The invariant lives in the schema, where it cannot be forgotten, rather than in a service method that every future caller must remember to invoke.

Money without a distributed transaction

No driver is dispatched until the fare is authorised. That sounds like it demands the very coordination the boundary rule was meant to avoid: the ride lives in one service, the payment in another, and one gates the other.

It does not, because of how the gate is expressed. The ride is persisted as REQUESTED — a state meaning awaiting authorisation and nothing else — and the rider gets their id back immediately. Authorisation proceeds independently. When the hold lands, the ride moves to WAITING_MATCH; if it is declined, it moves to a terminal failure state.

And matching reads only WAITING_MATCH. That is the whole gate. An unfunded ride is not rejected by a guard someone must remember to write — it is invisible to the code that would dispatch it.

Making the wrong thing unreachable beats checking for it.

A guard is a thing a future developer can forget, misplace, or refactor away. A query that simply never returns the wrong rows cannot be forgotten, because there is nothing to forget.

The two state machines then advance independently, neither waiting on the other. A late authorisation result is applied against whatever state the ride is now in, not the state it was in when the call was made. That distinction is what makes it not a distributed transaction: nothing is rolled back, because nothing was ever tentatively committed.

The one genuine race, and why it needed no sweep

A rider can cancel in the sub-second window while authorisation is still in flight. The cancellation event reaches the payment service perfectly well — it just arrives too early, when there is no hold to release yet. Handle it naively and it no-ops, and the hold lands seconds later against a dead ride.

The instinct is a reconciliation sweep: periodically find holds that look stranded and void them. That instinct is wrong, and the reason generalises. A hold that looks stale might belong to a genuinely long trip. Void it and the trip completes with nothing left to capture. The sweep would be inventing an answer it does not have.

The mechanism instead is to remember the intent: a cancellation arriving before the authorisation resolves sets a flag, and the authorisation voids on arrival rather than settling. Local to one service, no cross-service query, no timing guess. What remains is asserted in tests and watched by an alert — a human decides, because only a human has the missing context.

Two transports, one rule

There is a tempting story where Kafka arrives and services stop calling each other. It is a story the original project notes told, and it is wrong here — for a reason worth stating precisely, because it caught me out.

rider-service owns no data. So when a rider requests a ride, that request must reach matching-service synchronously: it needs a ride id back. A fire-and-forget event cannot return an identifier, a 409 for an illegal transition, or a 404 for a mismatched identity. Every actor-initiated command and every read stays a call, permanently.

Kafka carries propagation. Anything an actor waits on stays synchronous.

So the event backbone carries what genuinely fans out — audit capture, payment triggers, location streams, push notifications — and roughly fifteen thousand synchronous requests per second continue to flow through the internal API for the life of the project.

Those internal hops use gRPC rather than REST. On engineering grounds alone that is arguable: the bottleneck is Redis, not serialisation, and losing the ability to curl an internal endpoint costs something real when debugging five services. It was chosen anyway, deliberately, because the project's purpose is demonstrable experience and this is a gap worth closing. The public edge stays REST, so the façades become protocol translators.

One benefit turned out to be more than cosmetic. gRPC has a status code, ABORTED, meaning specifically you lost a race, a retry may succeed — distinct from FAILED_PRECONDITION, meaning this can never succeed. Two matching workers picking the same nearest driver is the first case; a rider cancelling a trip already underway is the second. Over plain HTTP both collapse into an indistinguishable 409, and the caller cannot tell whether retrying is sensible or futile.

Publishing without losing anything

If a service commits a state change and then publishes an event, those are two operations that can disagree. Commit succeeds, publish fails: the event is lost and downstream never learns. Publish succeeds, transaction rolls back: downstream acts on something that never happened.

The transactional outbox removes the gap. Domain code never publishes — it writes a row to an outbox table in the same transaction as the state change. A separate relay reads that table and ships the events. Either both happen or neither does, because it is one commit.

It also produced an unexpected simplification. Publishing becomes fire-and-forget by construction, so the migration from direct HTTP calls to Kafka stops being an architectural change and becomes a swap of the relay's dispatch class. Nothing in the domain knows or cares which transport is in play.

Two traps in the details

The outbox looks simple and hides two things that would each have caused a serious outage. I found neither of them myself.

The first is ordering. Rows carry a monotonically increasing identifier, so the natural relay reads "everything since the last id I saw". But sequence values are assigned when a row is inserted and become visible when its transaction commits — and those can happen in opposite orders. A transaction holding id 100 can commit after one holding 101. A relay tracking a high-water mark would step past 101, never see 100, and lose that event permanently — reintroducing the exact loss the outbox exists to prevent. The relay must therefore claim-and-delete rather than remember a position, so a late arrival is simply picked up next pass.

The second is a combination. Rows that fail repeatedly are marked dead and left in the table for inspection. Separately, the system sheds new requests when the outbox backlog grows too old, because a backlog that is not draining means events are not reaching anyone. Individually both are sound. Together, if the age metric counts dead rows, one poison message ages the backlog forever and every ride request sheds, permanently. A single bad event becomes a total outage.

Neither failure is visible in the component that causes it. Both live in the seam between two correct decisions.

Time, staleness, and refusing to invent answers

A driver whose phone dies is indistinguishable, from the server's point of view, from a driver in a tunnel. The system has to act on silence without knowing which it is — and the cost of acting wrongly is wildly different depending on what the driver was doing.

Driver was…Cost of deciding wronglyWindow
IdleMisses offers until they reappear; self-healingShort — 15 s
Driving to a pickupRider's driver is swapped mid-approach; visible, recoverableLonger — 90 s
Mid-tripTrip ended and the fare captured while it is still happening; unrecoverableLong — 10 min

Hence three windows rather than one shared constant. A single sixty-second timeout would end live trips every time a driver drove through a tunnel.

Crucially, none of these write to the driver's declared status. Losing signal does not end a shift; only the driver ends a shift. A driver who reappears becomes matchable again on their next heartbeat with no transition, no audit event and no manual recovery. A tunnel is not a resignation.

The decision I am most pleased with

An early design had a timeout on rides awaiting authorisation: if no outcome arrived within a few minutes, give up and fail the ride. It seems obviously right — the rider deserves an answer rather than waiting forever.

It is actively harmful, and the reason took a while to see. Failing the ride frees the rider to request another, which places a second hold while the first is still outstanding. During an outage that affects every stuck rider simultaneously — so a payment provider being briefly unreachable produces a wave of duplicate holds against the very provider that is already struggling. The timeout does not mitigate the failure; it amplifies it, and it fires hardest exactly when the system is least able to cope.

Removing it also revealed something that was already true: a rider with a stuck ride cannot request another one, because of the one-active-ride rule. That rule turns out to be natural admission control. During a payment outage the system throttles itself, with no explicit shedding — and the timeout would have deliberately opened that valve at the worst possible moment.

Bound the windows where the system can determine an outcome. Never bound a wait for external truth it will eventually receive.

"We looked for a driver and found nobody" is an answer the system genuinely holds, so giving up is legitimate. "We have not heard back about the payment" is not an answer — and inventing one is what causes the damage. A metric and an alert replace the mechanism; a human decides, because only a human has the context the system is missing.

What breaks first, and what happens then

Every queue in the request path has an explicit bound, and the tightest one sits where there is visibility — at the edge proxy, which has a stats page and can return a 503. The reasoning is that an unbounded queue does not absorb overload, it converts a throughput problem into an unbounded latency problem. And a bound set too generously at the edge does not help either: if the proxy admits a hundred concurrent requests to a service that can only run ten database operations at once, the queue has not been controlled, it has merely moved — into the JVM, where nothing reports on it.

Sizing them uses Little's Law: concurrency equals arrival rate times service time. Applied here it consistently says the numbers should be smaller than instinct suggests. Ride writes need single-digit database concurrency. The common reflex — setting a connection pool to a hundred "for safety" — is actively harmful, because it just relocates queueing into the database where each waiting connection costs real memory and nothing surfaces it.

Degrading on purpose

The final deployment target is a single local Kubernetes cluster, which makes the resource budget a real constraint rather than an abstract one. So it is worth knowing in advance what can be switched off — and the answer turned out to be already determined by the architecture.

A component can be turned off exactly when it sits behind an event boundary.

Audit and analytics consume events and nothing depends on them, so they can be stopped entirely and nothing breaks. Better still, nothing is lost: their consumer group offsets survive, and the backlog drains when they return. That is a direct consequence of Kafka never deleting on consumption — with a conventional acknowledge-and-delete queue, the events would have been gone unless the consumer stayed alive.

Payments are one step in: with the service down, rides stall before dispatch rather than crashing. Everything on the synchronous request path is required. Three tiers, and the boundary between them is not a judgement call — it follows the event boundaries exactly.

What I got wrong

Before adopting this design I put it through three independent adversarial reviews: one walking a quality rubric, one verifying every named technology and version against upstream sources, and one whose only instruction was to construct two components that obey every rule and still build something incompatible.

All three found real defects. That is the point of the exercise, but the pattern in what they found is the more useful lesson.

The worst findings were decisions where I had written down the reasoning and then failed to write down the rule.

The clearest example is the outbox ordering trap. I had explicitly noted that a cursor-based relay would skip late-committing rows — and then wrote a rule saying the identifier is "monotonic", which positively invites a developer to build exactly that cursor. The knowledge was recorded; the constraint was not. Nobody reading the rule would have known the hazard existed.

Others worth keeping:

  • Two rules that contradicted each other. One said the system could auto-authorise when payments were unavailable; another said rides should stall. The first would have dispatched rides against funds nobody was holding. Two reviewers found this independently, which is a good sign about both of them.
  • A restart that would have charged everyone. Redis holds no persistence by design, and driver positions carry an expiry. Nothing defined what a missing entry meant — so one reasonable reading has the system treat every driver as unreachable after a Redis restart and auto-complete every trip in progress, capturing every fare at once.
  • A recovery path that could never run. The window for detecting a silent driver mid-approach was longer than the window for giving up on finding a driver at all, with nothing saying when the second one started. A rescued ride would have been killed on the next sweep — not occasionally, but every single time.
  • A dependency that would not have compiled. The resilience library version I pinned predates the framework version I pinned, and does not publish a compatible module.

None of these were subtle once pointed out. All of them survived my own repeated reading, because I had spent hours arguing myself into each decision and could no longer see the gap between what I meant and what I had written. That is not a failure of care; it is a structural limit of reviewing your own work, and the only fix is someone who was not in the room.

The limits of this design

Knowing where a design stops working is more useful than believing it does not.

The geo index is a single key. Nearest-driver search runs against one Redis sorted set, and Redis executes commands on a single thread. Clustering does not help, because clustering shards by key and there is only one. The escape is partitioning the index by geographic cell — but that only pays when cells are substantially larger than the search radius, or boundary queries eat the gain. It is deliberately the last lever, not the first.

Matching is a single writer. One service owns all ride and dispatch state, which is what makes the concurrency story clean. It is also a ceiling: past a certain rate, the answer would be to shard by geography and accept the coordination that comes with it — which is roughly the point at which this architecture becomes a different architecture.

Tests run sequentially. They share one database and reset between classes, which is the right trade at this size and a real limit as the suite grows.

Ordering can break under retry. If one event for a ride fails and backs off while a later one succeeds, they reach consumers out of order. Consumer state machines reject invalid transitions, so the damage is contained rather than silent — but the guarantee is "detected", not "prevented". Closing it properly means blocking an entity's whole stream behind its own failed event, which costs more than it currently returns.

And none of the security shape is a security boundary. Identity is passed per request and trusted as-is, because authentication is explicitly out of scope. The 404-instead-of-403 convention, the sub-resource addressing, the masked payment tokens — those are correct habits practised deliberately, not defences. Presenting them as security would be dishonest, and the discipline is worth building precisely so it is automatic when the stakes are real.
